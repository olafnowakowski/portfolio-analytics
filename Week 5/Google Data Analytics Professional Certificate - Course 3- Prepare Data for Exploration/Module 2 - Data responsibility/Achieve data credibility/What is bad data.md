# Bad Data Sources

## Main Idea

Bad data sources are data sources that do **not** meet the ROCCC standards.

Good data should be:

- reliable
- original
- comprehensive
- current
- cited

Bad data fails one or more of these standards.

---

# Quick Review: ROCCC

## ROCCC Means

- **R** = Reliable
- **O** = Original
- **C** = Comprehensive
- **C** = Current
- **C** = Cited

If a dataset meets these standards, it is more likely to be good data.

If it does not meet these standards, it may be bad data.

---

# What Is Bad Data?

Bad data is data that is unreliable, incomplete, outdated, biased, uncited, or not original.

Bad data may also contain:

- human error
- inaccurate information
- missing information
- misleading visuals
- sample selection bias
- outdated values
- unclear sources

---

# Why Bad Data Is a Problem

Bad data can lead to:

- incorrect conclusions
- poor business decisions
- failed processes
- unreliable analysis
- risks to people or populations
- wasted time and resources

### Important Lesson

Every good solution depends on avoiding bad data.

---

# 1. Not Reliable

## Meaning

Bad data is **not reliable** when it cannot be trusted.

This may happen because the data is:

- inaccurate
- incomplete
- biased
- misleading
- not fit for use

---

## Example: Sample Selection Bias

A dataset may be unreliable if it has sample selection bias.

This happens when the data does not reflect the overall population.

### Example

If a survey only includes one type of person or one group, the results may not represent the full population.

---

## Example: Misleading Graphs

Bad data can also appear through misleading data visualizations.

A graph may make a change look bigger or smaller than it really is.

---

# Misleading Bar Graph Example

Two bar graphs can show the same interest rate data but create different impressions.

## Graph 1

The y-axis starts at **3.14%**.

This makes the change look very dramatic.

## Graph 2

The y-axis starts at **0**.

This shows that the interest rates actually stayed fairly flat.

---

## Important Lesson

Graphs can be technically based on real data but still be misleading if they are designed poorly.

---

# 2. Not Original

## Meaning

Bad data may not come from the original source.

If you cannot locate the original data source, you need to be careful.

---

## Why Original Sources Matter

Data from second-party or third-party sources may have been:

- copied
- changed
- summarized
- misinterpreted
- taken out of context

### Important Lesson

Whenever possible, validate data with the original source.

---

# 3. Not Comprehensive

## Meaning

Bad data is **not comprehensive** when it is missing important information needed to answer the question or solve the problem.

---

## Why This Matters

If the dataset does not contain all critical information, the analysis may be incomplete.

Incomplete data can lead to:

- weak conclusions
- wrong recommendations
- missed patterns
- poor decisions

---

## Human Error

Bad data may also contain human error.

Human error can happen when people:

- enter values incorrectly
- leave fields blank
- duplicate records
- use inconsistent formatting
- copy data incorrectly

---

# 4. Not Current

## Meaning

Bad data may be outdated or irrelevant.

Data becomes less useful as time passes, especially when the situation has changed.

---

## Example

A 10-year-old client list would not be useful for inviting current clients to a business event.

The list may include people who are no longer clients and may miss new clients.

---

## Current Data Sources

Many respected data sources refresh their data regularly.

This gives analysts more confidence that the information is up to date.

### Example

**Data.gov** provides access to U.S. government open data and is regularly updated.

---

# 5. Not Cited

## Meaning

Bad data may not be cited.

If a data source does not explain where the data came from, it is harder to trust.

---

## Why Citations Matter

Citations help analysts verify:

- who created the data
- where the data came from
- whether the source is credible
- when the data was updated
- whether the data can be trusted

### Important Lesson

If a source is not cited or vetted, it should not be used.

---

# Good Data vs Bad Data

| Good Data | Bad Data |
|---|---|
| Reliable | Not reliable |
| Original | Not original |
| Comprehensive | Missing important information |
| Current | Outdated |
| Cited | Not cited |
| Accurate | Inaccurate |
| Unbiased | Biased |
| Vetted | Not verified |

---

# Reliable Data Sources

Good data is often found from:

- vetted public datasets
- academic papers
- financial data
- governmental agency data
- U.S. Census Bureau data
- Data.gov

---

# U.S. Census Bureau

The **U.S. Census Bureau** is a strong example of a reliable data source.

It regularly updates information and provides quality data about:

- people
- economy
- population trends
- business activity

---

# Data.gov

**Data.gov** is a source for U.S. government open data.

It is useful because it provides access to current and regularly refreshed public datasets.

---

# Why Analysts Must Watch for Bad Data

Data Analysts must always check data quality before using it.

Bad data can create serious and lasting effects.

---

## Possible Impacts of Bad Data

Bad data can cause:

- one bad business decision
- repeated bad decisions
- failed business processes
- inaccurate reports
- misleading conclusions
- harm to populations
- loss of trust in analysis

---

# How to Avoid Bad Data

To avoid bad data, analysts should ask:

- Is the data reliable?
- Is the data original?
- Is the data comprehensive?
- Is the data current?
- Is the data cited?
- Is the source credible?
- Has the data been refreshed recently?
- Does the data contain bias?
- Does the data contain human error?
- Does the data answer the business question?

---

# Bad Data Warning Signs

Watch out for data that is:

- missing a source
- outdated
- incomplete
- biased
- not connected to the original source
- based on unclear collection methods
- visually misleading
- full of errors
- not relevant to the business question
- not vetted by a credible organization

---

# Big Lesson

Good data should ROCCC.

Bad data does not ROCCC.

Before using any dataset, Data Analysts need to check whether it is reliable, original, comprehensive, current, and cited.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Bad data sources do not meet the ROCCC standards.
- Bad data may be unreliable, incomplete, biased, outdated, uncited, or not original.
- Bad data can also contain human error.
- Unreliable data cannot be trusted.
- Misleading graphs can distort the meaning of data.
- Data should be validated with the original source whenever possible.
- Data must contain the critical information needed to answer the question.
- Current data is more useful than outdated data.
- Cited data is easier to verify.
- Bad data can lead to incorrect conclusions and poor decisions.
- Good data often comes from public datasets, academic papers, financial data, and government agencies.
- The U.S. Census Bureau and Data.gov are examples of reliable data sources.

---

# Extra Important Notes

- A graph can be misleading even when the numbers are technically correct.
- Starting a y-axis at a value other than zero can exaggerate differences.
- Second-party and third-party data are not automatically bad, but they require extra caution.
- Missing information can make a dataset less useful even if the existing values are accurate.
- Outdated data may not reflect the current situation.
- Uncited data should not be trusted without verification.
- Data credibility is essential for responsible analysis.

---

# Final Summary

Bad data sources are data sources that do not meet the ROCCC standards. They may be unreliable, not original, incomplete, outdated, or uncited. Bad data can also be wrong, biased, misleading, or filled with human error. For example, a misleading graph may exaggerate changes by starting the y-axis at a higher value instead of zero. Data Analysts must watch carefully for bad data because it can lead to incorrect conclusions, poor business decisions, failed processes, and harm to people or populations. To avoid bad data, analysts should use reliable sources such as vetted public datasets, academic papers, financial data, government agency data, Data.gov, and the U.S. Census Bureau. The main lesson is that good analysis depends on using data that is reliable, original, comprehensive, current, and cited.
