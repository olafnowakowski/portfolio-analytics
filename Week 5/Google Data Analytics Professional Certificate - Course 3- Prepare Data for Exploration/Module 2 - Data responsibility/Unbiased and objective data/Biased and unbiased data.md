# Sampling Bias

## Main Idea

**Sampling bias** happens when a sample is not representative of the population as a whole.

When sampling bias happens, the data can systematically favor one outcome and produce unreliable results.

---

# What Is Bias?

## Definition

**Bias** is a preference based on preconceived or subconscious ideas.

Bias can affect:

- people’s decisions
- data collection
- data analysis
- final conclusions

---

# What Is Data Bias?

## Definition

**Data bias** is an error that systematically skews results in a certain direction.

This means the data does not fairly represent the full population or situation being studied.

---

# What Is Sampling Bias?

## Definition

**Sampling bias** happens when the sample used in an analysis is not representative of the population as a whole.

A biased sample can lead to incorrect or unreliable conclusions.

---

# Population vs Sample

## Population

A **population** is the entire group you want to study.

## Sample

A **sample** is a smaller group selected from the population.

The sample should represent the population as fairly as possible.

---

# Example: Weather Preference Survey

## Scenario

There are 50 students in one class.

You want to know whether most of the class prefers:

- warm weather
- cold weather

You survey the first 10 students you meet.

Based on their responses, you conclude that the whole class prefers warm weather.

---

# Where the Bias Happens

The first 10 students you surveyed were all women.

This means the sample did not represent the entire class.

The survey excluded other gender identities in the class.

Because of this, the results may not reflect the real preferences of all 50 students.

### Important Lesson

A sample that only includes one part of a population can create biased results.

---

# Why Random Sampling Helps

## Random Sampling

**Random sampling** means selecting participants so that every part of the population has an equal chance of being included.

Random sampling helps reduce sampling bias.

---

## Why It Works

If the sample is chosen randomly, it is more likely to include different parts of the population.

This can include differences in:

- gender identity
- age
- background
- preferences
- experiences
- location
- other relevant characteristics

---

# Unbiased Sampling

## Definition

**Unbiased sampling** produces a sample that is representative of the population being measured.

This means the sample reflects the larger group more fairly.

---

# Biased Sample vs Unbiased Sample

| Sample Type | Meaning | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Biased sample** | Does not represent the full population | Can skew results |
| **Unbiased sample** | Represents the population fairly | Produces more reliable results |

---

# How Sampling Bias Affects Analysis

Sampling bias can cause analysts to:

- draw incorrect conclusions
- overrepresent one group
- underrepresent another group
- miss important patterns
- make poor recommendations
- produce unreliable results

---

# Visualizing Sampling Bias

Visualizations can help analysts identify whether a sample is biased.

In the class example, you could create two bar charts:

1. A bar chart showing the gender identities of the entire class.
2. A bar chart showing the gender identities of the surveyed students.

Then you could compare the two charts.

---

# Why Visualizations Help

Visualizations make it easier to see whether the sample matches the population.

If the sample chart looks very different from the population chart, there may be sampling bias.

### Important Lesson

Visualizations can help reveal misalignment between a sample and the full population.

---

# Example Visualization Idea

## Population Chart

Shows all 50 students by gender identity.

## Sample Chart

Shows the 10 students surveyed by gender identity.

## What to Check

Ask:

- Does the sample reflect the full class?
- Are some groups missing?
- Are some groups overrepresented?
- Are some groups underrepresented?

---

# How to Avoid Sampling Bias

To reduce sampling bias:

- use random sampling when possible
- make sure all parts of the population have a chance to be included
- compare the sample to the full population
- check for missing groups
- use visualizations to identify sample misalignment
- avoid relying only on convenient or easy-to-reach participants

---

# Convenience Sampling Risk

Surveying the first people you meet may be convenient, but it can create bias.

This is because the first people available may not represent the full population.

### Important Lesson

Easy-to-reach data is not always representative data.

---

# Big Lesson

Sampling bias can make data unreliable because it causes the sample to favor one part of the population.

A good sample should represent the population as fairly as possible.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Bias can come from conscious or subconscious preferences.
- Data bias can systematically skew results.
- Sampling bias happens when a sample does not represent the population.
- Random sampling helps reduce sampling bias.
- In random sampling, all parts of the population should have an equal chance of being included.
- A biased sample can favor one outcome.
- An unbiased sample represents the population being measured.
- Visualizations can help reveal whether a sample is aligned with the population.
- Bar charts can be used to compare the full population with the sample.
- Analysts should check for underrepresented or excluded groups.
- Convenience samples can create biased results.

---

# Extra Important Notes

- A sample does not need to include every person, but it should fairly represent the population.
- If one group is overrepresented, the results may be skewed.
- If one group is excluded, the analysis may miss important information.
- Sampling bias can happen even if the analyst does not intend it.
- Visual checks are useful for spotting sample imbalance.
- Reliable analysis starts with fair data collection.

---

# Final Summary

Sampling bias happens when a sample does not fairly represent the population being studied. In the class weather preference example, surveying only the first 10 students created bias because all of them were women, meaning other gender identities were not represented. This could lead to an unreliable conclusion about the preferences of the whole class. Random sampling helps avoid this problem by giving all parts of the population an equal chance of being included. Visualizations, such as bar charts comparing the full population to the sample, can also help reveal whether the sample is misaligned. The main lesson is that Data Analysts must choose samples carefully so their data is fair, representative, and reliable.
