# Types of Data Bias

## Main Idea

There are many types of data bias.

Bias can affect how data is:

- collected
- observed
- interpreted
- analyzed
- understood

When data is biased, the results can be unreliable because they are systematically skewed in a certain direction.

---

# Quick Review: Sampling Bias

## Definition

**Sampling bias** happens when a sample is not representative of the population as a whole.

---

## Example

If you are researching commuters and only survey people walking on the sidewalk, your sample misses people who:

- ride bicycles
- drive cars
- take the subway
- use buses
- work remotely

This means your sample does not include all sides of the story.

---

## Why Sampling Bias Is a Problem

Sampling bias can lead to incorrect conclusions because the sample does not represent the full population.

### Important Lesson

To avoid sampling bias, make sure the sample represents the population being studied.

---

# Other Types of Data Bias

This lesson introduces three additional types of data bias:

1. **Observer bias**
2. **Interpretation bias**
3. **Confirmation bias**

Together with sampling bias, these biases affect how people collect and make sense of data.

---

# 1. Observer Bias

## Definition

**Observer bias** is the tendency for different people to observe things differently.

It is also sometimes called:

- experimenter bias
- research bias

---

# Observer Bias Example: Scientists

Scientists often use observations to collect data.

For example, two scientists might look at bacteria under the same microscope but notice or record different things.

This difference in observation is observer bias.

---

# Observer Bias Example: Blood Pressure

Observer bias can also happen during manual blood pressure readings.

Because blood pressure meters are sensitive, different healthcare workers may get different readings.

They may round readings up or down to the nearest whole number.

---

## Why This Is a Problem

If doctors consistently round blood pressure readings up or down, then:

- health conditions may be missed
- patient data may become inaccurate
- medical studies may use imprecise data
- conclusions may become unreliable

### Important Lesson

Observer bias can affect the accuracy and precision of collected data.

---

# 2. Interpretation Bias

## Definition

**Interpretation bias** is the tendency to interpret ambiguous situations in a positive or negative way.

This happens when people understand the same situation differently based on their background, experiences, or expectations.

---

# Interpretation Bias Example: Voicemail

## Scenario

You receive a voicemail from your boss asking you to call her back.

You assume she sounds angry and that you are in trouble.

But when your friend listens to the same voicemail, they think your boss sounds calm and straightforward.

---

## Why This Is Interpretation Bias

You and your friend heard the same message, but interpreted it differently.

Your interpretation was influenced by your personal history with your boss.

Your friend interpreted it differently because they did not have the same background or relationship.

---

## Why This Is a Problem in Data

If personal interpretations are added into analysis, the results may become biased.

### Important Lesson

The same data can be interpreted differently by different people.

---

# 3. Confirmation Bias

## Definition

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to search for or interpret information in a way that confirms preexisting beliefs.

In simple terms:

**People see what they want to see.**

---

# How Confirmation Bias Happens

Someone may already have a belief or gut feeling.

Then they may only notice data that supports that belief while ignoring data that challenges it.

---

# Everyday Examples

Confirmation bias can happen when people:

- read news from sources that already agree with their views
- socialize mostly with people who share their opinions
- ignore information that challenges their beliefs
- focus only on evidence that supports what they already think

---

# Why Confirmation Bias Is a Problem

Confirmation bias can lead to bad analysis because it causes analysts to ignore important signals.

This can result in:

- incomplete analysis
- misleading conclusions
- poor decisions
- bad recommendations
- unreliable results

### Important Lesson

Good analysis requires looking at all evidence, not only evidence that supports your expectations.

---

# Four Types of Data Bias Covered

| Bias Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sampling Bias** | The sample does not represent the full population | Surveying only sidewalk pedestrians when studying all commuters |
| **Observer Bias** | Different people observe things differently | Two scientists seeing different things under a microscope |
| **Interpretation Bias** | People interpret ambiguous situations differently | Hearing the same voicemail as angry or calm |
| **Confirmation Bias** | People search for or interpret information to confirm existing beliefs | Only noticing data that supports a gut feeling |

---

# What These Biases Have in Common

These types of bias are different, but they all affect how people:

- collect data
- observe data
- interpret data
- make sense of data

### Important Lesson

Bias can enter the analysis process at many different points.

---

# Why Analysts Must Watch for Bias

Data Analysts need to be alert for bias because biased data can lead to poor decisions.

Bad data makes it difficult to do good analysis.

Bias can affect:

- accuracy
- trustworthiness
- fairness
- conclusions
- recommendations
- business decisions

---

# Inspecting Data

No matter what kind of data you use, it should be inspected for:

- accuracy
- trustworthiness
- bias
- completeness
- quality

---

# How to Reduce Bias

To reduce bias, analysts should:

- use representative samples
- collect data from multiple perspectives
- avoid relying only on personal interpretation
- check assumptions
- look for evidence that challenges the original idea
- document how data was collected
- inspect data for accuracy and trustworthiness
- ask whether any groups or perspectives are missing

---

# Big Lesson

Bias can affect both the data itself and the way people interpret it.

A good Data Analyst stays alert for bias and checks data carefully before making conclusions.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Sampling bias happens when a sample does not represent the whole population.
- Observer bias happens when different people observe things differently.
- Interpretation bias happens when people interpret the same situation differently.
- Confirmation bias happens when people search for or interpret data to confirm existing beliefs.
- Bias can affect data collection and analysis.
- Biased data can make results unreliable.
- Analysts need all sides of the story to avoid biased conclusions.
- Data should always be checked for accuracy and trustworthiness.
- Good analysis requires questioning assumptions.
- Analysts should be alert for many forms of bias throughout their work.

---

# Extra Important Notes

- Observer bias is also called experimenter bias or research bias.
- Interpretation bias is especially common when situations are unclear or ambiguous.
- Confirmation bias can cause analysts to ignore data that contradicts their expectations.
- Sampling bias can happen when only convenient participants are included.
- Avoiding bias helps make analysis more fair, accurate, and reliable.
- The biases covered in this lesson are only a few examples of the many types of bias analysts may encounter.

---

# Final Summary

This lesson explains several types of data bias. Sampling bias happens when a sample does not represent the full population. Observer bias happens when different people observe the same thing differently, such as scientists looking through a microscope or healthcare workers reading blood pressure. Interpretation bias happens when people interpret the same situation differently based on their experiences, such as hearing a voicemail as either angry or calm. Confirmation bias happens when people look for or interpret information in a way that supports what they already believe. These biases all affect how data is collected and understood. The main lesson is that Data Analysts must constantly watch for bias and inspect data for accuracy and trustworthiness before drawing conclusions.
