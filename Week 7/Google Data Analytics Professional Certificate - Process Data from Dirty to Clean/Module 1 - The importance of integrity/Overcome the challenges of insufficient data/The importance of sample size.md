# Sample Size and Representative Data

## Main Idea

Data Analysts need the right kind and the right amount of data to meet a business objective.

When collecting data from an entire population is too difficult, expensive, or time-consuming, analysts can use a sample.

A good sample should represent the larger population as accurately as possible.

---

# 1. Population

A **population** is all possible data values in a certain dataset.

In data analysis, the population includes every person, item, event, or record that could be studied.

## Example

If you are studying cat owners in Canada, the population would include all cat owners in Canada.

---

# 2. Why Analysts Do Not Always Use the Full Population

Using 100% of a population is ideal, but it is not always realistic.

Collecting data from an entire population can be:

- too expensive
- too time-consuming
- difficult to organize
- impossible to access completely

## Example

A global organization wants to know what kinds of toys cat owners in Canada prefer.

There may be millions of cat owners in Canada, so collecting data from every single one would be very difficult.

---

# 3. Sample

A **sample** is a part of a population that is used to represent the whole population.

The goal of using a sample is to collect enough information from a smaller group so the analyst can make predictions or conclusions about the larger group.

---

# 4. Sample Size

**Sample size** refers to the number of people, records, or data points included in the sample.

A larger and well-chosen sample can help increase confidence that the results represent the population.

## Example

Instead of surveying millions of cat owners in Canada, an analyst might survey hundreds or thousands of cat owners.

---

# 5. Why Sample Size Matters

Sample size helps determine how confident analysts can be that their conclusions represent the full population.

A good sample size can make analysis:

- faster
- less expensive
- more practical
- easier to manage

---

# 6. Benefits of Using a Sample

Using a sample can be helpful because it:

- saves time
- reduces cost
- makes large studies more practical
- allows analysts to make estimates about a population
- avoids the need to collect data from every individual

If the sample is chosen carefully, it can still produce useful and reliable insights.

---

# 7. Risk of Using a Sample

Using a sample can create uncertainty.

Because the analyst is not using the entire population, they cannot be 100% sure that the sample perfectly represents the population.

This is why sampling must be done carefully.

---

# 8. Sampling Bias

**Sampling bias** happens when a sample is not representative of the population as a whole.

This means some members of the population are overrepresented or underrepresented.

---

# 9. Sampling Bias Example

Suppose an analyst surveys cat owners about their favorite cat toys.

If the survey only includes people with smartphones, then cat owners without smartphones are excluded.

This means the sample does not fully represent all cat owners.

## Problem

The results may reflect only the preferences of smartphone users, not all cat owners.

---

# 10. Overrepresentation and Underrepresentation

Sampling bias can happen when certain groups appear too often or not often enough in the sample.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Overrepresented | A group appears too much in the sample |
| Underrepresented | A group appears too little or not at all in the sample |

---

# 11. Random Sampling

**Random sampling** is a way of selecting a sample from a population so that every possible type of sample has an equal chance of being chosen.

Random sampling helps reduce sampling bias.

---

# 12. Random Sampling Example

For cat owners in Canada, a random sample should give different types of cat owners an equal chance of being selected.

For example:

- cat owners in apartments in Ontario
- cat owners in houses in Alberta
- cat owners with smartphones
- cat owners without smartphones
- cat owners from different age groups
- cat owners from different income groups

Each type should have a fair chance of being represented.

---

# 13. Why Random Sampling Helps

Random sampling helps make the sample more representative.

It reduces the chance that the analysis will only reflect one group within the population.

This makes the final conclusions more reliable.

---

# 14. Sample Size and Business Objectives

The sample should match the business objective.

Before using a sample, analysts should ask:

- What population am I studying?
- Does this sample represent that population?
- Are any groups missing?
- Are any groups overrepresented?
- Is the sample large enough?
- Does the sample help answer the business question?

---

# 15. Analysts and Sample Creation

Creating sample sizes often happens before the analyst receives the data.

However, analysts should still understand whether the data they are analyzing is representative.

Even if the analyst did not create the sample, they should be aware of possible sampling issues.

---

# 16. Common Mistakes to Avoid

## Mistake 1: Assuming a small sample represents everyone

A small sample may not represent the full population.

## Mistake 2: Ignoring who is missing from the sample

If certain groups are excluded, the results may be biased.

## Mistake 3: Using convenience instead of random sampling

Using only easily available data can create bias.

## Mistake 4: Forgetting the business objective

The sample should support the question the analyst is trying to answer.

## Mistake 5: Being too confident in sample results

A sample can support conclusions, but it usually cannot provide 100% certainty.

---

# 17. Key Takeaways

- A population includes all possible data values in a dataset.
- A sample is a smaller part of a population.
- Sample size is the number of people, records, or values included in the sample.
- Samples are used when collecting data from the entire population is too difficult, expensive, or time-consuming.
- A good sample should represent the population.
- Using a sample is more cost-effective and faster than collecting data from everyone.
- Sampling can create uncertainty because it does not include the full population.
- Sampling bias happens when a sample does not represent the population as a whole.
- Random sampling gives every possible type of sample an equal chance of being chosen.
- Random sampling helps reduce sampling bias.
- Analysts should make sure the data they use is representative and aligned with the business objective.
