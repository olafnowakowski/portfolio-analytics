# Sample Size: Key Terms and Guidelines

## Main Idea

Before choosing a sample size, it is important to understand key terms such as population, sample, margin of error, confidence level, confidence interval, and statistical significance.

Sample size affects how accurate and reliable your analysis can be.

A larger sample usually gives more accurate results, but it also costs more time, money, and effort.

---

# 1. Population

**Population** means the entire group you are interested in studying.

## Example

If you are surveying people in your company, the population is all employees in your company.

If you are studying voters in a city, the population is all eligible voters in that city.

---

# 2. Sample

**Sample** means a subset of the population.

A sample is only part of the full group, like a small taste of something larger.

## Example

If your company is too large to survey every employee, you can survey a representative sample of employees instead.

The goal is for the sample to reflect the larger population as accurately as possible.

---

# 3. Margin of Error

**Margin of error** is the expected difference between the sample result and the result you would get if you surveyed the entire population.

Because a sample is only part of the population, the result may not be exactly the same as the full population result.

## Important Lesson

The smaller the margin of error, the closer the sample result is expected to be to the true population result.

---

## Example

If a survey result says 60% of employees support a new policy with a margin of error of 5%, the actual population result may be somewhere around 55% to 65%.

---

# 4. Confidence Level

**Confidence level** means how confident you are in the survey results.

## Example

A 95% confidence level means that if you ran the same survey 100 times, you would expect similar results in 95 of those 100 surveys.

---

## Important Lesson

Confidence level is chosen before the study begins.

It affects the margin of error and the sample size needed.

A higher confidence level usually requires a larger sample size.

---

# 5. Confidence Interval

**Confidence interval** is the range of possible values where the population result is likely to fall.

It is calculated as:

```text
Sample result +/- margin of error
```

---

## Example

If the sample result is 60% and the margin of error is 5%, the confidence interval is:

```text
55% to 65%
```

This means the true population result is likely somewhere in that range, based on the confidence level.

---

# 6. Statistical Significance

**Statistical significance** helps determine whether a result is likely real or could be due to random chance.

The greater the statistical significance, the less likely the result is due to chance.

---

## Important Lesson

Statistical significance helps analysts decide whether a pattern or result is meaningful enough to trust.

---

# 7. Minimum Sample Size

A common rule is:

```text
Do not use a sample size smaller than 30.
```

A sample size of 30 is often considered the minimum because it is the smallest sample size where the average result of a sample starts to represent the average result of a population.

---

# 8. Why Minimum Sample Size Is 30

The recommendation for a minimum sample size of 30 is based on the **Central Limit Theorem**, or **CLT**.

The Central Limit Theorem says that as sample size increases, the results more closely resemble a normal, bell-shaped distribution.

A sample size of 30 is commonly treated as the smallest size where this idea begins to work reliably.

---

# 9. Regression Analysis and Sample Size

Researchers who use **regression analysis** also often prefer a minimum sample size of 30.

Regression analysis is used to study relationships between variables.

For example, it can help determine how one variable may affect another.

---

# 10. Common Confidence Levels

The most commonly used confidence level is:

```text
95%
```

In some cases, a 90% confidence level may also be acceptable.

---

# 11. When to Increase Sample Size

You should increase sample size when you need:

- a higher confidence level
- a smaller margin of error
- greater statistical significance
- more accurate results
- stronger support for an important decision

---

# 12. Sample Size and Business Problems

Sample size depends on the business problem.

A sample size that is acceptable for one business question may not be acceptable for another.

---

## Example: City Library Survey

Suppose a city has a population of 200,000 people.

A sample of 200 people might be enough if the goal is to understand how residents feel about a new library.

But a sample of 200 might not be enough if the goal is to predict how residents would vote to fund the library.

---

# 13. Why Stakes Matter

The higher the stakes, the more careful you need to be with sample size.

## Lower-Stakes Example

Finding out how residents feel about a new library may allow for a larger margin of error.

## Higher-Stakes Example

Predicting how residents will vote to fund the library requires more accuracy, so it may need a larger sample size.

---

# 14. Accuracy vs Cost

Larger sample sizes usually give more accurate results.

But larger samples also cost more.

They may require more:

- time
- money
- staff
- tools
- data collection effort

---

# 15. Cost-Benefit Trade-Off

Analysts must weigh the cost of collecting more data against the benefit of more accurate results.

---

## Example: Consumer Preferences

A company studying consumer preferences for a new product line may not need a very large sample.

A smaller sample may be good enough and more cost-effective.

---

## Example: Drug Safety

A company studying the effects of a new drug would need a much larger sample.

For drug safety, the benefits of accuracy are much greater than the cost of collecting more data.

---

# 16. Sample Size Calculators

A **sample size calculator** is a tool that helps determine the appropriate sample size for a study.

You can enter:

- population size
- desired confidence level
- desired margin of error

The calculator then estimates the sample size needed.

---

# 17. Why Sample Size Calculators Are Useful

Sample size calculators help analysts avoid guessing.

They use statistical formulas to estimate how much data is needed to achieve the desired confidence level and margin of error.

---

---

# 19. Example: Small vs Large Sample Decision

| Business Question | Possible Sample Size Need | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| How do residents feel about a new library? | Smaller sample may be acceptable | Lower-stakes opinion question |
| How will residents vote to fund the library? | Larger sample likely needed | Higher-stakes decision with real funding impact |
| What products do customers prefer? | Moderate or smaller sample may be enough | Useful for business direction, but not usually life-critical |
| Is a new drug safe? | Large sample needed | High-stakes health and safety decision |

---

# 20. Common Mistakes to Avoid

## Mistake 1: Using a sample size below 30

A sample smaller than 30 may be too small to represent the population reliably.

## Mistake 2: Ignoring the business problem

The sample size should match the importance and risk of the decision.

## Mistake 3: Choosing a sample size only based on cost

Lower cost is helpful, but the sample still needs to be large enough to support useful conclusions.

## Mistake 4: Forgetting margin of error

A small sample can create a larger margin of error.

## Mistake 5: Confusing confidence level and confidence interval

Confidence level is how confident you are.

Confidence interval is the range of likely values.

## Mistake 6: Assuming one sample size works for every project

Sample size varies depending on the study, population, objective, risk, and available resources.

---

# 21. Questions to Ask When Choosing Sample Size

Before choosing a sample size, ask:

- What is the population?
- What is the business problem?
- How important is the decision?
- What confidence level is needed?
- What margin of error is acceptable?
- How much statistical significance is needed?
- What sample size can the budget support?
- What happens if the analysis is wrong?
- Can a sample size calculator help?
- Is the sample representative of the population?

Sample size is not one-size-fits-all.

The right sample size depends on the business problem, the desired confidence level, the acceptable margin of error, the need for statistical significance, and the cost of collecting more data.
